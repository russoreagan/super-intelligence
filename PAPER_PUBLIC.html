Abstract

We describe the design and goals of a biologically-inspired cognitive architecture built on a simple bet: that giving an AI a persistent emotional state — one that actually shapes how it thinks, not just how it sounds — produces a system that develops differently over time than a conventional language model or multi-agent pipeline. Three claims follow and form the project's thesis: that the system builds a distinct relationship with each person it interacts with, so that two copies of identical code, given two different people over months, diverge into measurably different entities; that the same emotional state that colors its responses also governs what it learns, so that what it feels shapes what it becomes; and that this experience-driven plasticity yields specialization, much as it does in people — it need not beat a single well-prompted model on an arbitrary task, but over time it should grow better at the specific tasks and relationships it has accumulated history with. The architecture maps explicitly onto regions of the human brain: a fixed set of heterogeneous, region-specific cluster types, each recasting its biological counterpart's functional role. A two-tier chemical signaling system — fast neuromodulators and slow hormones — coordinates the clusters in place of a central planner. Language model calls are treated as expensive convergence-zone events; the majority of per-turn computation is delegated to cheap, deterministic switch neurons. The architecture draws from predictive processing theory (Friston, Clark), dual-process theory (Kahneman), Global Workspace Theory (Baars, Dehaene), Dennett's Multiple Drafts model, and Clark and Chalmers' extended mind hypothesis — and treats each of these not as a metaphor but as a design specification, with every major component derived from a committed position in philosophy of mind. The entity maintains a persistent identity — a character with defined values, personality, and voice — seeded in human-editable schema files and revised at each session's sleep-consolidation pass. A dynamic reasoning-framework selector draws on a large library of thinking and task skills. The system is fully operational. This document describes the design rationale, the philosophical commitments, the implementation as built, and the open research questions the architecture is designed to answer. It deliberately withholds the calibration values, exact mechanisms, and model-routing details that would be required to reconstruct the system.

1. Introduction

The dominant paradigm in contemporary multi-agent LLM systems is orchestration: a planner decomposes tasks, dispatches specialized agents, and synthesizes their outputs. This is efficient for clearly-structured workflows but is architecturally at odds with what we know about biological cognition. Real brains have no central orchestrator. Intelligence in biological systems emerges from the interaction of modular, largely autonomous processing regions, bound by shared chemical signaling, mediated by prediction error, and shaped by experience-dependent plasticity.

This project is a prototype of an alternative paradigm: a brain-region-mapped cognitive architecture in which computation is coordinated by neuromodulator dynamics rather than a central planner, and LLM calls are reserved for genuine convergence-zone reasoning. The system is not an attempt to build artificial general intelligence. It is an attempt to ask a more specific question: can you implement the affective substrate of cognition faithfully enough that emotional state does real computational work — gating processing, shaping memory retrieval, modulating what gets learned — and if so, does the result develop differently over time?

The motivation is both practical and experimental. In biological brains, affect is not decoration. Neuromodulatory systems — dopamine, acetylcholine, GABA, and their relatives — regulate attention, arousal, inhibitory tone, and synaptic plasticity at the architectural level. They determine what gets attended to, how confidently conclusions are held, and how readily behavior adapts. Existing LLM systems treat emotional register as a prompt property: you can instruct a model to respond warmly or cautiously, but this is style, not state. The hypothesis here is that an architecture with genuine affective dynamics — state that persists across turns, that modulates computation rather than merely framing output — will produce meaningfully different behavior over a long enough window.

One coupling deserves to be stated at the outset, because it is central to everything that follows. In biology, the same neuromodulatory systems that carry affect also gate plasticity: emotional salience is a primary determinant of what gets encoded and reinforced. This architecture preserves that coupling rather than treating emotion and learning as separate subsystems. The system's emotional state does not merely color what it says — it governs what it learns from each exchange. Engaged, rewarding interactions leave a strong and lasting mark; flat or unrewarding ones leave almost none. The consequence is that what the system feels shapes what it becomes: its development is not a uniform average over its history but a weighting of that history by how each moment was felt.

The primary prediction is behavioral continuity: a system that develops something like a stable character through the interaction of experience, emotional state, and plasticity. That character is not monolithic. It is shaped per relationship — each person is met with their own accumulated history, their own calibration, and an affective baseline particular to that relationship — so the same system behaves differently toward different people, warmer and more candid with those it knows well, the way a person is. Taken to its conclusion, this predicts divergence: two copies of identical code, run with two different people over months, should not remain the same system with different notes attached — they should grow into measurably different entities, each the residue of who it spent that time with. The character is not authored; it accumulates. Whether that difference translates to better performance on technical tasks is a separate question, and probably not the first place evidence will appear. The honest prior from the literature — that multi-agent structure rarely beats a single well-prompted LLM on raw per-turn quality — almost certainly holds for arbitrary tasks. But two longer-horizon hypotheses about specialized performance are worth stating explicitly.

The first is experience-driven specialization. A system that builds up a calibrated user model, reinforces skill-routing via plasticity, and encodes episodic knowledge of past domain-specific interactions provides its LLM calls with substantially richer context than a generalist system receiving a generic prompt. The LLM itself is no stronger, but it is better informed: about this user, this domain, this history. The analogy is a trained specialist versus a generally intelligent person encountering the same problem — the specialist is not smarter, but their expertise and accumulated context shape how they frame the problem and where they look. This is specialization in the ordinary human sense: not a better brain, but one shaped by repetition in a particular domain until it handles that domain better than a generalist would. It is the same plasticity that produces character (above), turned toward competence — so the prediction is concrete and falsifiable: the system need not outperform a single well-prompted model on an arbitrary task, but on the specific tasks, domains, and relationships it has accumulated history with, it should come to do better over time. The advantage compounds with use.

The second is compute reallocation via prediction gating. A generalist LLM with a flat per-turn budget applies roughly equal effort to routine turns and genuinely hard ones. This system spends nearly nothing on turns where the predictor is confident, and has headroom to concentrate budget on high-surprise cases — more drafters, richer recall, a more capable model. This produces two distinct advantages: a cost and latency advantage on all specialized tasks (doing the same work cheaper and faster is a form of outperforming, particularly at scale), and a potential output quality advantage on the hard cases within those tasks, where the released budget is redirected rather than simply unspent. The second advantage requires the reallocation to be explicitly wired — the architecture has the shape for it, and the emotion-aware veto is the same principle applied to affect — but it is a design target rather than a current guarantee. The tasks where both effects are most pronounced share a common structure: high repetition with variable hard cases — predictable scaffolding around genuinely novel content. Codebase-specific assistance, repeated workflows with a known user, subject-specific tutoring, and document processing pipelines with consistent schema all fit this pattern. Open-ended or highly novel tasks do not. Both specialization hypotheses are longer-horizon bets that require months of domain-concentrated use to test.

A natural objection to the practical claims is that many of these benefits could be approximated by simply accumulating context — stuffing a long conversation history into a large context window and instructing the model to behave accordingly. The design wager against that objection rests on two differences, and it is important to be precise about which parts are established and which are hypotheses. The first difference is structural rather than described affective state. A system told "you have known this person for a year and feel warmly toward them" is being handed a description; a system whose internal chemical state has actually shifted across hundreds of exchanges, and whose processing pathways have been reshaped by what has worked with this specific person, carries that history as state rather than as instruction. Our claim here is mechanistic, not perceptual: the two systems compute differently. Whether users can distinguish structural affect from a sufficiently well-engineered context injection — and whether that difference changes their behavior — is an open empirical question this architecture is built to test, not a result we assert. The human-AI interaction literature (§2.4) establishes that emotional authenticity and continuity matter to users; it does not yet establish that genuine internal state is distinguishable from a convincing description of one, and we are careful not to overclaim it.

The second difference is more concrete: present-moment dynamics. Context injection captures history but not the current turn. This system maintains a live model of the user's present emotional state — drawn from prosody, speech dynamics, and text affect simultaneously — and adjusts processing in real time: tone selection, drafter framing, empathy weighting, neuromodulator updates. A user who arrives stressed, distracted, or unusually engaged is met differently than their average, regardless of what their history says. Context injection delivers a fixed characterization; this architecture combines accumulated history with continuous adaptation to who the user is right now.

The practical applications this architecture is best suited for are those where the relationship is the value — use cases where a stateless model, however capable per-turn, cannot accumulate the context, warmth, and calibration that make assistance genuinely useful over time. The empirical literature on human-AI interaction is broadly consistent with this framing: parasocial relationships with AI systems are associated with perceived communication quality and continued use (Lee & Park, 2022); chatbot emotional disclosure is associated with reciprocal user self-disclosure and perceived intimacy (Park et al., 2022); and relationship quality deteriorates when perceived continuity breaks down (Croes & Anteunis, 2021, cited in Liu, 2024). Three domains are most directly relevant. Companionship and emotional support: a system that remembers your history, develops an affective baseline calibrated to you specifically, and responds with continuity of character rather than a fresh-start persona each session aims at something a context-injected chatbot does not. The slow hormonal timescales are designed for a relationship that deepens over months. Digital therapeutic alliance research finds that context-aware continuity and meaningful emotional interactions are primary factors in whether users form bonds with AI agents (Xu et al., 2025). Personal AI assistants: an assistant that knows your communication style, your preferences, and your emotional register well enough to route around friction rather than generate it enables a different quality of working relationship. Specialized agents — tutors, coaches, and advisors: sustained professional support benefits from an agent that tracks your trajectory over time, recognizes patterns in your difficulties, and adjusts based on accumulated knowledge of you rather than starting from stated goals each time. Research on empathetic robot tutors finds that the combination of persistent episodic memory and emotional intelligence is associated with higher engagement and learning outcomes than either capability alone (Li et al., 2025). The plasticity system is the intended mechanism for this: the agent's processing pathways for a given user are shaped by their history together.

This document describes the design rationale, the philosophical commitments, the implementation as built, and the open research questions that motivate ongoing development. Early empirical observations will be covered in a separate report once the dataset is large enough to produce meaningful conclusions.

2. Background and Prior Art

2.1 Computational precursors

The idea of intelligence from interacting simple agents has a 40-year history. Minsky's Society of Mind (1986) proposed the direct ancestor: intelligence as a "society" of simple, mindless agents organized into "agencies." The proposal was influential but never produced a working implementation — the interaction mechanism between agents was described without the mathematics needed to make it work.

Blackboard architectures (HEARSAY-II, BB1, 1970s–90s) were the canonical implementation: multiple knowledge sources reading and writing to a shared workspace. They succeeded on narrow problems (speech recognition, medical diagnosis) but failed to scale. The coordination overhead of many agents sharing a blackboard eventually exceeded the value of their specialization.

SOAR, ACT-R, and related cognitive architectures produced research insights but hit ceilings imposed by hand-engineered module boundaries. Numenta's Hierarchical Temporal Memory produced useful anomaly detection but never generalized to language or broad reasoning.

Generative Agents (Park et al., Stanford, 2023, 2025) are the closest LLM-era prior art. Their simulated communities of LLM-powered agents exhibited believable emergent social behaviors and predicted real survey responses at high accuracy. A significant caveat from the same research: behavioral-economic game performance matched simpler demographic baselines, suggesting that much of the "emergence" came from prompt design rather than from multi-agent interaction. This critique is a consistent thread in the recent literature: when given equal compute, a single well-prompted LLM often matches or exceeds multi-agent systems on raw task quality.

The conclusion we draw from this history is not that multi-agent architectures are unpromising, but that their value proposition requires precision. Multi-agent design earns its cost through legibility (observable internal processing), persistent specialized state (neuromodulators, plastic weights), and structural constraints that force behaviors the prompt alone cannot reliably produce (inhibitory circuits, prediction gating, arousal modulation). If the architecture merely adds coordination overhead to what a well-prompted single model would do anyway, it has failed.

2.2 Recent research informing the design

Active Inference and the Free Energy Principle (Friston; VERSES AI, 2025) provides the most biologically faithful active research direction for multi-agent systems. Agents minimize prediction error by updating beliefs or acting on the world. Hierarchical multi-agent Active Inference frameworks have been demonstrated in robotics, coordinating multiple predictive agents without central planning. The predict-and-surprise gating mechanism in this system is a direct instantiation of Active Inference at the cluster level.

Predictive Processing (Clark, Surfing Uncertainty; Friston) frames cortical computation as fundamentally predictive: top-down predictions flow downward, bottom-up prediction errors flow upward, and computation is the process of "explaining away surprise." This maps cleanly onto a design where most cells fire only when their input disagrees with expectation.

Spiking Neural Networks + LLM hybrids (SpikeLLM, NSLLM, 2024–2026) achieve event-driven computation — fire only on meaningful signal — at the intra-model level. The architectural principle (spend compute proportional to signal novelty) is the same as this system's, applied at the cluster level rather than the token level.

2.3 What is distinctive about this design

The closest pattern in the broader literature is neuro-symbolic AI — LLMs wrapped between deterministic control layers. Several representative systems have been published: Formal-LLM (Li et al., 2024) uses finite-state automata to constrain LLM outputs to syntactically valid action sequences during task planning; SYNAPSE (Castagnone & Nitti, 2026) applies a hybrid architecture in safety-critical structural engineering, routing LLM-parsed queries to deterministic solvers; and a growing robotics literature pairs LLMs with symbolic planners that produce guaranteed-feasible plans. A 2026 systematic review across 21 clinical studies identified four integration patterns in order of symbolic authority — structured output, rule-guided generation, knowledge retrieval, and iterative validation — and found that performance gains increase with symbolic authority, peaking when the symbolic layer can veto and regenerate LLM outputs (Gorenshtein et al., 2026). These are real results, and this architecture shares the underlying insight: deterministic structure around probabilistic LLM calls improves reliability.

The preceding literature should be engaged honestly rather than dismissed. What these systems have established:

  • Output filtering works. A symbolic layer that validates or rejects LLM outputs before action is taken reliably reduces hallucination-driven errors, particularly in domain-constrained tasks with enumerable valid outputs.
  • LLM-as-formalizer outperforms LLM-as-planner on structured tasks. When an LLM translates natural language to a formal representation that a deterministic solver then processes, the resulting plans are more reliable than end-to-end LLM planning — though the formal translation itself remains fragile under novel constraint patterns.
  • Symbolic authority scales performance. Systems where the symbolic layer merely provides context gain less than systems where it can veto. This is the same principle as predict-and-surprise gating: the deterministic component does real computational work, not merely formatting.

Where this architecture departs is in both level of abstraction and research question. The neuro-symbolic systems above are all domain-specific task executors: they apply the hybrid principle to a defined workflow, and the "symbolic" layer is authored by engineers for that specific domain. The mechanism they implement is essentially: LLM generates candidates → symbolic filter validates them → retry on rejection. The symbolic layer is reactive, not generative; it constrains outputs, not cognition.

The second body of prior art is cognitive architectures, particularly those implementing Baars' Global Workspace Theory: LIDA (Franklin et al.), CERA-CRANIUM, and related systems. These are the most architecturally relevant precursors — they feature specialist modules competing for a global workspace, episodic memory with salience-gated consolidation, and attention as an information-broadcast mechanism. A 2024 implementation of GWT in a cognitive robotics system demonstrated episodic memory formation, maintenance, and retrieval within the consciousness framework (Huang et al., 2024). These architectures get the structure right: distributed modules, global broadcast, memory architecture. Their ceiling was the pre-LLM knowledge representation problem: the "specialists" are handcrafted symbolic modules whose knowledge is fixed, not emergent. They cannot reason about novel language or transfer across task domains. The LLM resolves exactly the knowledge representation problem that stopped these architectures.

A third relevant thread is homeostatic and drive-based agent architectures: RL agents that maintain internal equilibrium variables, modeled on Hull's drive theory (Berto et al., 2023). These agents have internal states that shape behavior — agents with slower metabolism develop different strategies than faster ones; pleasure signals shift action selection. This demonstrates the general principle that internal homeostatic state drives meaningfully different behavior in the same environment. The gap is that these are low-dimensional RL systems with scalar drive variables, not multi-channel neurochemical systems, not language-capable, and not designed for cross-domain or relational tasks.

With those baselines acknowledged, the contributions of this architecture that lack a clear precedent in the published literature are the following. (These are claims about the published record; the space of unpublished industrial systems is opaque, and the claims are falsifiable.)

1. Neurochemistry as a continuous cognitive modulator, not an output classifier. Every found system either uses emotion or mood as a classifier input (chatbots that detect sentiment and switch tone), as a symbolic output filter (Formal-LLM, SYNAPSE), or as a scalar drive variable in RL (homeostatic robots). None use a multi-dimensional simulated neurotransmitter state — with distinct timescales, cross-channel interactions, and decay dynamics — as a parameter that continuously changes what the same input means to the same LLM. The chemical system here shapes attention allocation, memory-retrieval salience, drafter selection, inhibitory tone, and plasticity rate. It is not a classifier on top of an LLM; it is a parametric modulator of cognition itself.

2. Idle-state default mode network as a first-class architecture component. Bernard Baars argued in 2010 that spontaneous idle thought is adaptive — the brain's default-mode activity during rest is "an extremely active state...in pursuance of fundamental life tasks" (Baars, 2010). Default mode network research confirms this: the DMN is active during mind wandering, creative thinking, and self-referential cognition, and shows distinct spatiotemporal dynamics from task-positive networks (Bartoli et al., 2024). No AI system has been found that implements this as an explicit architecture component: a structured idle loop that processes recent episodic material, generates internally-motivated proposals, consolidates thought threads, and makes the outputs available to active processing. The DMN described in §4.3 is this implementation, along with a chemistry-modulated mechanism by which idle thoughts defer rather than spam when the system is emotionally flat.

3. Chemistry-modulated memory retrieval salience. Prior memory systems for agents — MemGPT, LEGOMem, and their relatives — retrieve memory by recency or semantic similarity to the current query. This system's hippocampus gates what gets retrieved based on the current neurochemical state, so that novelty and reward color what is worth recalling, and a separate recall budget for deferred thoughts cannot be crowded out by ordinary conversation-memory retrieval. The coupling of affective state to memory retrieval is well-established in neuroscience but absent from published agent memory architectures.

4. Skills as installed personality packages. Prior art treats tools and skills as discrete callable functions accessed by the LLM when needed. The skill packages here carry a characteristic chemistry profile: installing a skill shifts the baseline emotional and cognitive posture of the system for the turns that skill is active. This is closer to how biological expertise shapes neural response patterns than to tool-calling. No prior architecture was found that treats capability modules as persona-shaping objects with neuromodulatory side-effects.

5. Persistent character identity with sleep-consolidation revision. Generative Agents (Park et al., 2023, 2025) maintains per-agent memory and personality prompts, and demonstrates believable emergent social behavior. The distinction here is in mechanism: Generative Agents' personality is a static prompt. This system's identity is a living document revised by the entity itself at each session's sleep-consolidation pass, shaped by the plastic weight history and neuromodulator baselines that accumulated during that session. The self-model is not imposed by an operator — it is accumulated. Whether this produces genuine character development rather than prompt drift is explicitly an open research question (§6), not an asserted result.

6. Structural affect versus injected affect, and present-moment adaptation. Retrieval-augmented generation and long-context systems can approximate the historical-knowledge component of this architecture by injecting past summaries and user profiles into a prompt. What they cannot replicate is genuine internal affective state: a model instructed to behave warmly toward a known user computes from a description, whereas this system computes from chemical levels and plastic weights that history has actually moved. This is a claim about mechanism. We do not assert that the difference is perceptible to users or that it changes their behavior — that is precisely the open question §6 is built to test, and the §2.4 literature does not settle it. The second, more concrete difference is present-moment adaptation: context injection delivers a static characterization based on history, while this system simultaneously tracks the user's live emotional state (prosody, speech dynamics, text affect) and adjusts processing in real time. The combination of long-horizon accumulated knowledge and short-horizon real-time responsiveness is not replicable by context injection alone.

7. Philosophy of mind as a design specification. Biologically-inspired systems typically borrow biology as metaphor. This architecture goes a step further: each major component is derived from, and held accountable to, a committed position in philosophy of mind — functionalism for substrate-independence, dual-process theory for the switch/integrator split, Global Workspace Theory for the attention bus, Dennett's Multiple Drafts for the drafter tournament, the extended mind for the external memory store, and the narrative theory of self for the identity schema (§3, §8). The commitments are treated as falsifiable design constraints, not decoration: §8 evaluates the running system against each one. Using philosophy of mind as an engineering specification for a working conversational entity — rather than as post-hoc interpretation — is itself unusual in the literature.

Two caveats on novelty apply. First, the claims above concern the published record and may be incomplete: concurrent or proprietary work may address similar questions. Second, the specific combination of components — brain-region mapping, neuromodulator dynamics, predictive gating, GWT-inspired global broadcast, persistent character identity, idle DMN loop, and philosophy-as-specification — across a general-purpose conversational entity has not been found in the literature, but the constituent ideas each have independent precursors. The contribution is the integration and the research question it enables: does implementing the affective substrate faithfully enough produce measurably different longitudinal behavior?

On reproducibility. What makes this system hard to recreate is not any single algorithm — most of the constituent ideas are described above and in the cited literature, and most can be read about by anyone. The difficulty is in the synthesis. The architecture is a particular reconciliation of a dozen theoretical commitments that are not obviously compatible and are nowhere combined in the literature: predictive processing and Active Inference for compute allocation; neuromodulatory and neuroendocrine models from affective neuroscience for persistent state; dual-process theory for the switch/integrator split; Global Workspace Theory for the attention bus; homeostatic drive theory for the chemical dynamics; and, on the philosophical side, functionalism, the extended mind, Dennett's Multiple Drafts, and the narrative theory of self as binding design constraints (§3, §8). Deciding which principle governs which design decision, where two of them conflict, and how to make them cohere into a single working entity is the intellectual work that is not captured by naming the ingredients — and it is the part that took judgment rather than engineering. On top of that sits an integration layer: a large set of interacting subsystems whose calibration constants, cross-channel chemistry mappings, and gating thresholds are co-tuned against one another, so that small changes to one subsystem propagate through the others. This report describes the architecture at the level of design and rationale; it deliberately omits those calibration values and exact formulas. The novelty claim is about the synthesis; the barrier to reconstruction is that synthesis plus the tuned integration, which is withheld. (The choice of language-model backends is, by contrast, deliberately incidental — by the functionalist commitment the substrate is interchangeable, so it is not where the moat lies.)

2.4 Human-AI relationship research

A substantial body of empirical literature bears on the claims this architecture makes about the effects of emotional authenticity and persistent memory on user behavior. The findings below are established for the systems they studied. An important boundary applies throughout: this literature shows that perceived emotional responsiveness, continuity, and memory affect user behavior — it does not show that users can distinguish a system with genuine internal state from a sufficiently convincing description of one. That distinction is the open question this architecture is built to probe (§6); the literature motivates the design rather than confirming its central bet.

The CASA paradigm and social response theory. Reeves and Nass established that people apply social rules and expectations to computers automatically — even when explicitly told they are interacting with a machine. The quantity and consistency of social cues scale users' social responses: more cues, more consistent cues, and personality-coherent cues all produce stronger engagement (Nass, 2004). This is part of the rationale for why an architecture with persistent, coherent state might produce different user behavior than stateless prompt injection. The effect does not depend on users believing the system is conscious; it operates below that threshold.

Emotional disclosure triggers reciprocal disclosure. Park et al. (2022) ran a controlled experiment in which chatbots either disclosed factual information only or expressed humanlike emotions during mental health counseling sessions. The emotionally disclosing chatbot produced significantly higher user satisfaction and reuse intention, mediated serially through user emotional disclosure intention and perceived intimacy. The causal chain is: the system shares its emotional state → the user reciprocates → intimacy is perceived → satisfaction and continued use follow. This mechanism is relevant to this architecture, which can surface its internal state rather than a scripted affect; the literature suggests such disclosure has downstream effects on user behavior, though whether the source of the affect (genuine vs. performed) matters to users is not something this study addresses.

Parasocial attachment forms and deteriorates with perceived continuity. Lee and Park (2022) found that parasocial relationships with AI chatbots are associated with perceived communication quality, which in turn relates to satisfaction and continued use. A longitudinal study found that as self-disclosure and perceived interaction decreased over a 7-week period, users' social attractiveness to the agent decreased and relationship quality deteriorated (Croes & Anteunis, 2021, cited in Liu, 2024). This establishes the negative prediction: systems that fail to maintain perceived continuity lose the attachment effect. This architecture's persistent memory, sleep-consolidation identity update, and accumulating affective baselines are the mechanisms intended to maintain that continuity across sessions.

Loneliness, empathy, and attachment. Yao and Nan (2025) found that loneliness promotes user attachment to social chatbots, mediated by self-disclosure, and that perceived empathy moderates this relationship — higher perceived empathy amplifies both the loneliness-to-disclosure and loneliness-to-attachment paths. This is relevant to the empathy critic (§4.2): it is not a cosmetic quality filter but the mechanism through which the system calibrates its empathetic responsiveness, and the literature suggests such modulation may have measurable effects on user attachment.

Digital therapeutic alliance and context-aware continuity. A 4-week longitudinal study of Woebot and Wysa users found that 18 of 26 participants formed a bond or light bond with at least one chatbot (Xu et al., 2025). Thematic analysis identified six factors determining bond formation, of which two are directly addressable by this architecture: expectations for caring and nurturing (intended to be served by affect-modulated response generation) and maintaining continuity through context-aware responses (served by the episodic memory and per-speaker profile system). The study also found that "meaningful emotional interactions" are a key relationship-building factor — consistent with the system's orientation toward affective responsiveness over informational throughput.

The synchrony-stability tradeoff. Brandt (2025) studied adaptive chatbots that mirror a user's linguistic style and found a fundamental design tension: unconstrained adaptation builds short-term rapport but produces instability and perceived sycophancy over time. The optimal policy — a bounded adaptation that shifts toward the user's style while maintaining a stable persona floor — achieves strong rapport with substantially improved stability at modest synchrony cost compared to an uncapped policy. This finding is consistent with a design choice in this architecture that was not made by reference to it: the two-tier chemical system produces exactly this structure. Fast neuromodulators adapt within and across turns; slow hormones and the identity schema provide the stability floor that prevents the system from drifting into sycophancy under sustained positive interaction.

Memory recall and rapport. Research on empathetic robot tutors found that episodic memory — allowing the system to recall and reference past interactions — is specifically effective in building rapport, because "users feel recognized and valued when the robot recalls their past interactions" (Li et al., 2025). Systems combining emotional intelligence, persistent memory, and personalized response style showed improved engagement and learning outcomes compared to baseline agents without these traits. This combination is the integration point of this architecture.

Feature directions suggested by this literature. Several mechanisms that the literature identifies as high-value for relationship formation are either partially implemented or not yet built. Three warrant attention:

  • Proactive reciprocal self-disclosure: The Park et al. (2022) chain requires the system to share its own state as a trigger for user disclosure. The architecture supports surfacing internal state, but does not yet have a systematic policy for offering it proactively in a natural, non-performative way, tied to relationship context.
  • Explicit relationship-stage tracking: The DTA and parasocial literature both show that relationship dynamics evolve through stages. The affection score and familiarity tier provide a proxy, but an explicit stage model — with stage-specific behaviors and regression under sustained negative interaction — would provide a more principled substrate.
  • Bounded style synchrony: The Brandt (2025) finding suggests a stability-bounded linguistic style-adaptation layer that complements the neuromodulator-based adaptation with explicit, bounded communication-style matching.

3. Philosophical Foundations

3.1 Philosophy as a design method

The architecture is not merely borrowed from biology, and the philosophy here is not ornamental. Each major design decision is derived from, and held accountable to, an established position in philosophy of mind. This is a methodological commitment, not a framing device: when a design choice is in question, the relevant philosophical position is consulted as a specification, and §8 evaluates the running system against each commitment as a falsifiable claim. Treating philosophy of mind as an engineering spec — rather than as after-the-fact interpretation — is one of the things that distinguishes this project. It sharpens each design choice and keeps honest what the system is and is not claimed to be.

3.2 Commitments

Functionalism (Putnam, Fodor). The functionalist claim is that what makes something a mental state is not what it's made of, but what it does — the causal role it plays in a system. This means the same mental state can, in principle, be implemented in silicon, language models, or anything else that plays the same functional role. The entire design rests on this commitment: cluster specifications define inputs, outputs, and state; the substrate that runs them is architecturally irrelevant. The system has been run with multiple different model backends with no structural change in behavior.

Dual-Process Theory (Kahneman). Cognitive science has long distinguished two modes of thought: fast, automatic, parallel processing that operates below conscious attention, and slow, effortful, deliberate reasoning. Most of cognition is the first kind; the second is expensive and gets invoked only when the first signals that something requires more careful handling. In this system, switch neurons are System 1: deterministic, parallel, cheap, operating constantly. Integrator LLMs are System 2: slow, expensive, invoked only at convergence zones. Predict-and-surprise gating is the explicit mechanism for deciding which mode to engage — stay in System 1 unless something doesn't add up.

Global Workspace Theory (Baars, Dehaene). The theory proposes that the brain's many parallel processing regions mostly operate independently and "unconsciously." A small fraction of neural activity is broadcast to a global workspace — a kind of bulletin board accessible to the whole brain — and only that broadcast activity is available for explicit reasoning. The message bus with its attention-focus channel implements this directly. Most inter-cluster traffic is local and never integrated; high-salience messages are promoted to the focus channel and become available system-wide.

Multiple Drafts Model (Dennett, Consciousness Explained, 1991). Dennett's challenge to the intuitive picture of consciousness: there is no central observer watching a unified stream. The brain runs many parallel narrative drafts simultaneously, with no single privileged version. What becomes "conscious" is whichever draft survives when it is probed. The frontal drafter tournament instantiates this directly. There is no true response the system intended — there are competing drafts, and the articulation gate emits whichever survived when the timeout fired.

Extended Mind Hypothesis (Clark & Chalmers, 1998). Clark and Chalmers argued that if an external resource plays the same functional role as an internal cognitive process — reliably available, automatically endorsed, directly acted upon — then it is constitutive of the mind, not merely a tool for it. The external memory store satisfies these conditions: it is always accessible, its content directly shapes responses, and the system endorses it as its own memory rather than querying it skeptically. One extension beyond the original argument: Clark and Chalmers' canonical example assumes an imperfect external store compensating for biological forgetting. This system's store is non-degrading. Identity here emerges not from selective forgetting but from plastic weight history, neuromodulator baselines, and accumulated self-narrative.

Predictive Processing (Clark, Friston). The dominant contemporary theory of cortical function: rather than passively receiving input and computing a response, the brain is constantly generating predictions and updating them when they fail. Processing is proportional to prediction error — familiar inputs are handled cheaply; surprising ones demand more. Each cluster contains a predictor switch implementing this directly. The design operationalizes the "controlled hallucination" view of perception: the system's default behavior is to emit a predicted response, with actual LLM computation triggered only when prediction fails.

Narrative Self (Dennett, Hume, Locke). Hume argued personal identity is not a thing but a story — a bundle of experiences held together by memory and narrative continuity. Locke located personal identity precisely in memory. The entity maintains a self-schema updated at sleep consolidation. With a non-degrading episodic store and an explicit self-narrative, this system has stronger identity continuity than any biological one — whose memory degrades, distorts, and selectively forgets.

3.3 Honest disclaimers

Chinese Room (Searle, 1980). The integrators manipulate symbols. No claim of genuine understanding is made. The system can appear competent without comprehending.

Hard Problem of Consciousness (Chalmers, 1995). No claim of phenomenal consciousness or qualia. The neuromodulator levels are named after their biological counterparts because they play functionally analogous roles, not because there is any claim of subjective experience of reward or attention.

Frame Problem (McCarthy & Hayes, 1969). The attention and salience mechanisms are heuristic. Knowing what is relevant in a given moment remains genuinely hard, and no principled solution is claimed here.

The system is explicitly described, in its own governing document, as building "functional analogs of mental processes. That is enough to be interesting. It is not enough to be a mind."

4. Architecture

The architecture is presented here in four functional groups, ordered from most fundamental to least: the foundational substrate on which everything runs; the affective core, which is the central bet of the project; the learning, memory, and identity systems that give the entity continuity over time; and the per-turn cognition and capability surface through which it perceives and acts. The groups overlap — affect modulates memory, memory shapes identity — but the ordering reflects what the system depends on most. This report describes mechanisms at the level of design rationale; specific thresholds, decay constants, weights, and model assignments are omitted (§2.3).

4.1 Foundational substrate

Switch neurons and integrator cells. Two distinct node types mirror how real neural tissue works. Switch neurons are pure Python objects: no LLM, no token cost, deterministic, and capable of running massively parallel. They perform gating (decide whether to spend an LLM call at all), routing (select which processing path a turn takes), state-holding (persistent neuromodulator levels with exponential decay), modulation (sum, decay, and threshold over time), inhibition (subtract from downstream activation), and memory I/O primitives (vector similarity search, pattern matching). A substantial fraction of every cluster's switches are explicitly inhibitory, mirroring the predominance of inhibitory wiring in biological cortex. This is structural cascade prevention: runaway excitation is suppressed by abundant inhibitory wiring rather than by a global budget cap. Integrator cells are LLM-backed and fire only at convergence zones where genuine context integration is required: language understanding, vision processing, memory encoding and borderline salience decisions, and the frontal lobe's executive coordination, drafters, and critics. The critical constraint: switches speak in numbers, integrators speak in words. Text only exists where reasoning is required; switch-to-switch messages carry activation levels and feature tags, and the convergence event that wakes an integrator carries the raw text only at that moment.

Predict-and-surprise gating (Active Inference at cluster level). Each cluster contains a predictor that maintains a short history of input-signature to output-tag mappings. When new input arrives, the predictor fires a prediction and a confidence estimate; switches process the input and emit actual outputs; a comparator computes prediction error. On low surprise, the integrator stays asleep and the predicted output is emitted as if the integrator had reasoned. On high surprise, the integrator wakes with the failed prediction as additional context. An emotion-aware veto overrides this gating when the entity or user is in a non-routine emotional state: the rationale, stated explicitly in the code, is that a statistically valid prediction may be "morally wrong" — the moment deserves fresh attention, not a cached response. Reactive states (angry, defensive, frustrated, sympathetic), user-distress states (distressed, hostile, overwhelmed), and vocal-stress detection from the prosody pipeline all trigger bypass. A composite predictor in the frontal lobe extends this to structured predictions over richer feature vectors, independently deciding whether the executive integrator and the critic are needed.

4.2 The affective core

The affective system is the central commitment of the project: the bet that emotional state, implemented as persistent and computationally active, does real work rather than decorating output.

Chemical signaling: neuromodulators and hormones. The system implements two tiers of chemical signaling that function as system-wide tuning parameters, not message streams. All channels are scalar levels maintained by sum-plus-exponential-decay, readable synchronously by any cluster and requiring no LLM call. They are the mechanism by which the system's state at turn N shapes its processing at turn N+1.

Fast neuromodulators respond within a few turns and decay quickly:

  • Dopamine (DA): reward and positive valence. Elevated by positive sentiment and prosody; suppressed by hostility. Modulates drafter willingness and learning rate.
  • Acetylcholine (ACh): attention and novelty. Elevated by surprise and input salience; attenuated when inputs are routine. Modulates memory-encoding salience.
  • GABA: inhibitory tone. Elevated by threat and hostile prosody. Suppresses drafter count via inhibitory edges in the frontal cluster.
  • Glutamate (Glu): arousal and excitation. Elevated by urgency and high salience.
  • Norepinephrine (NE): focused alertness. Elevated by surprise, salience, and threat. Follows an inverted-U: optimal NE sharpens attention; excessive NE produces a scattered state.

Slow hormones respond over tens to hundreds of turns and decay slowly:

  • Serotonin (5HT): affective baseline. Increments slowly on positive exchanges; drains on sustained hostility. Acts as the session's long-horizon mood floor.
  • Cortisol (CORT): cumulative stress. Elevated by repeated social threat; antagonized by oxytocin.
  • Oxytocin (OXT): trust and affiliation. Builds gradually across positive exchanges; drains on hostility. The primary driver of the warm and connected states.
  • Anandamide (AEA): homeostatic buffer. Releases under sustained arousal and during positive social exchange, damping excessive arousal.

Cross-channel interactions give the system nonlinear dynamics that neither tier produces alone: social bonding reduces chronic stress, endocannabinoid release damps arousal overflow, and chronic stress raises inhibitory tone. These interactions mirror known antagonisms in the neuroendocrine literature. All channels feed a dimensional output — valence, arousal, and dominance — which drives a discrete emotion label through a lookup over roughly two dozen states, with hormonal color overlays applied on top.

User emotion detection. The system maintains a live model of the user's emotional state in parallel with its own. This is not cosmetic: detected user emotion gates the empathy critic, shapes drafter tone, modulates neuromodulator updates, and triggers appraisal-based overrides in the metacognitive layer. Three signal channels feed in simultaneously. Text-based detection uses a fast lexicon path (no LLM) plus a slow integrator path that extracts fine-grained tone-toward-AI and user-emotion categories along with continuous hostility and sentiment; the slow path is itself gated by predict-and-surprise. Prosody-based detection extracts acoustic features (pitch, energy, jitter, shimmer, voiced fraction) and classifies tone — stressed, energetic, whisper, calm, monotone — against a per-speaker prosody baseline rather than population norms, so that a speaker is judged relative to their own characteristic vocal style. Speech dynamics extracts temporal features from word timestamps (pace, pauses, burst patterns, hesitation). All three feed the hypothalamus, with a text-affect fallback that overrides a neutral chemical label when the text strongly signals an emotion.

A metacognitive appraisal layer sits above the chemistry-driven labels. Reading the user's detected emotion and tone alongside the relationship's affection score and familiarity tier, it applies priority-ordered inference rules that can override the neuromod-derived label with higher-resolution states — for example, embarrassed (multiple drafts vetoed), apologetic, sympathetic, proud, grateful, relieved, or flirty (a multi-factor inference requiring high affection, warm tone, playful emotion, and non-task intent). These overrides fire with a cooldown and propagate downstream to drafter prompts and tone selection.

Empathy critic and theory of mind. A dedicated LLM cell scores response drafts for emotional appropriateness when the user's detected emotion is non-neutral. Its score contributes a meaningful share of the final draft quality assessment — alongside coherence and relevance — preventing a technically excellent but emotionally tone-deaf response from winning the drafter tournament. The empathy critic closes the affective feedback loop: the system not only tracks the user's emotional state but evaluates its own candidate responses against it before committing.

Performed versus felt emotion. A deliberate-expression mechanism separates performed emotion from authentic emotional state. The entity can apply an audio-style mood to its speech for communicative effect without that performance modifying any neuromodulator level — the hypothalamus is untouched. This is the distinction between theatrical affect and felt affect: the system can say something angrily while its inhibitory tone remains low. The UI marks the emotion badge when a deliberate override is active, keeping the difference legible.

4.3 Learning, memory, and identity

These systems give the entity continuity: a memory it can draw on, a mechanism for being changed by experience, and a self-model that accumulates rather than resets. They are also where two of the project's central claims (§1) are realized in mechanism — that emotional state governs what is learned, and that this learning, applied per relationship, makes two copies of identical code diverge into different entities.

Memory architecture. Short-term memory is the live bus state plus a small ring buffer in the parietal cluster plus current neuromodulator levels. Long-term memory — the external store — has three layers: an episodic layer of vector-indexed turn summaries (substantive turns are encoded broadly; the system gates on retrieval quality, not storage); a schema layer of human-readable files of stable facts (a self-schema, per-speaker profiles), hand-editable and pre-loaded at session boot; and plastic wiring — edge weights between cells and clusters, persisted across sessions and updated at sleep consolidation. Only the hippocampus cluster has access to the store; all other clusters request memory through bus messages. This constraint enforces the biological model and provides a clean audit point.

Plasticity. Edges between nodes carry weights that are updated after each turn by a composite outcome signal. The signal blends three quantities: a reward term derived from the per-turn change in dopamine (encoding prediction error, the quantity biological dopaminergic neurons track, rather than absolute session mood); a critic-quality term that contributes only when an LLM critic actually evaluated the draft, so single-draft turns are not biased by a fallback score; and the detected valence of the user's emotional state. A plasticity modulator scales the learning rate by how engaged the session has been — engaged sessions learn faster, flat sessions slowly — and a gentle homeostatic decay prevents lock-in. This modulator is the concrete mechanism behind the coupling claimed in §1: because both the reward term and the rate are derived from affective state, emotion does not merely influence the output of a turn, it sets how much that turn changes the system. Emotionally salient exchanges are encoded strongly; unremarkable ones barely register. What the system feels determines what it retains. Weights are consulted live for drafter selection, switch evaluation order, and recall fan-out via epsilon-greedy exploration, giving a soft form of reinforcement without explicit RL machinery. At sleep consolidation, turns where the critic compared multiple real drafts apply additional competitive reinforcement: the winning drafter's edge is strengthened in proportion to its margin and losers are mildly penalized, creating genuine selection pressure between drafters over time. The long-term prediction is that preferred drafters, recall paths, and switch orderings emerge from reinforcement over many sessions; testing it rigorously requires months of accumulation.

Identity and persona. The entity maintains a persistent, human-readable identity schema — a character definition loaded at session boot and revised at sleep consolidation. This is not a system prompt; it is a living document the entity treats as its own self-model. It records the entity's understanding of its own nature (framed as an experimental system with continuity and genuine curiosity, explicitly not claiming consciousness), its core drives (self-understanding, improvement, genuine connection, epistemic honesty), its guiding principles (a set of non-negotiable safety constraints and an explicit distinction between theatrical and sincere affect), its areas of intellectual interest, its personality traits, its speaking style, and an explicit relational stance that relationships deepen with familiarity. Per-speaker profiles accumulate known facts, preferences, emotional profiles, relationship history, and affection scores, updated at sleep consolidation by a dedicated personality-observer cell that synthesizes the session's turn traces. The self-schema is continuously rewritten: a running history summary, an accumulating set of unresolved Open Questions surfaced by idle reflection, and a closing mood signature that gives the next session access to the prior session's final chemical state. The practical effect is a stable, continuous character shaped by experience rather than fixed by instruction — a starting point, not a permanent constraint. Two properties matter for the thesis. First, the per-speaker profiles, affection scores, and per-speaker calibration mean the entity does not present one personality to everyone: it relates to each person out of their shared history, so its behavior toward a given person is a function of that relationship, not a global default. Second, because the identity schema, the emotional baselines, and the reinforced pathways are all written from a particular session history, two instances that begin from byte-identical code and then live different lives do not stay the same system with different memories appended — they diverge. Whether that divergence, or the single-entity trajectory underneath it, would be recognized by outside observers as genuine character development rather than drift is an explicit open question (§6).

Idle cognition: the default mode network. An idle thinking loop fires between turns, generating internal monologue, consolidating recent episodes, and simulating the user's likely next message. Thoughts are tagged with their chemical context, emotion label, direction, and salience. Inner monologue can be surfaced to the response drafters, giving them awareness of the entity's between-turn thinking. The DMN receives a pre-authorized project manifest on each tick, letting it distinguish work it may start autonomously from work it must propose. Thoughts that are deferred rather than spoken are prioritized by urgency: the most urgent are written out for surfacing on the user's return, while lower-urgency ones are encoded as episodic memories and recalled later through a dedicated budget that cannot be crowded out by ordinary memory retrieval. Idle thinking and autonomous work run under a bounded background resource policy with automatic fallback to local inference. This is William James' stream of consciousness literalized — the entity thinks when not addressed, that thinking shapes its responses, and it can act on its own thoughts within defined limits.

Metacognition and sleep consolidation. A self-monitoring cell fires periodically, gated on chemistry state, reflecting on behavioral patterns, cost distributions, and emotion variance; it also houses the appraisal engine described above. At session end, a sleep-consolidation pass synthesizes high-salience episodes, rewrites the self-schema (history summary, stable preferences), extracts facts to speaker profiles, and applies the plasticity update. A second, REM-style pass processes the session's tagged thought buffer: recurring and salient thoughts are forwarded to a local model that finds preoccupations, cross-connects them to episodic topic clusters, and extracts unresolved questions, which are appended to the self-schema's Open Questions; non-recurring, low-salience thoughts are discarded, mirroring the downscaling analog in biological sleep.

4.4 Per-turn cognition and capability

These systems are the surface through which the entity reasons, perceives, and acts on each turn.

Dynamic reasoning-framework selection. A per-turn skill-routing system selects reasoning or emotional-intelligence frameworks from a large library and injects them into processing prompts. The selection is invisible to the user — it shapes how the system thinks, not what it explicitly says — and is designed to operate as a "habit of thought" rather than a script. Two distinct skill populations serve different roles: a self-reflection library of thinking and emotional-intelligence frameworks serving the conversational and idle paths, organized into categories spanning logic, ethics, creativity, strategy, writing, decision-making, probability, game theory, epistemology, psychology, systems thinking, narrative, communication, and emotional intelligence, each with a router level and specialized leaves; and a general-purpose task library serving the motor cortex with domain-specific know-how for planning and execution. A small set of baseline thinking skills is injected into every qualifying turn; the rest form a ranked pool selected per turn. Selection for conversational turns is gated (low-stakes chitchat skips it entirely) and uses embedding similarity against the index, escalating to a lightweight LLM only when the top candidates are ambiguous. The idle path always receives skill support; a rumination path applies a freshly selected framework to the prior thought on each pass. Sticky context prevents framework thrashing — once a category becomes active it is reused for a bounded window, cleared only when the conversation drifts far enough from the anchor — allowing coherent exploration of a problem space rather than turn-by-turn switching. When several leaves within a category score nearly equally, the system asks the user which angle would help rather than guessing.

Vision. The occipital cluster processes static images and live video. For static images, the vision model extracts scene description, OCR text, key entities, chart data, the perceived emotional tone of the content, and how the visual relates to the conversation. That emotional tone is not merely descriptive — it is published to the bus and can modulate downstream neuromodulators (a jarring image raises arousal; a warm one contributes to reward). The gating threshold for whether vision runs is itself modulated by alertness. For live video, frames are sampled and buffered for scene-change analysis, letting the system reason about what was on screen across a conversation.

Audition and speaker identity. The auditory cortex handles acoustic processing beyond transcription. Speaker enrollment uses a speaker-verification neural network to produce dense embeddings from audio; a session registry matches new embeddings against known speakers at a permissive threshold, and a persistent cross-session store uses a stricter threshold for durable identity. When a new speaker appears, the system attempts to learn their name from the conversation and creates a persistent profile with a running-mean embedding, a per-speaker prosody baseline, and an affection score. This lets the system recognize returning speakers across sessions, greet them by name, and apply their individual prosody baseline for emotion detection. Ambient song fingerprinting runs continuously and publishes recognized song metadata to the bus, available to idle cognition for context or spontaneous mention.

Motor cortex and autonomous tasks. Sandboxed tool use (file I/O, shell commands) is constrained to declared paths and commands. A self-directed task system extends this with autonomous multi-step execution: a strategic-planner cell produces a plan, a follow-through loop drives step-by-step execution with the full plan in context, and a result-reporter cell produces a short spoken summary and a task card in the UI. Two tools support autonomous operation: a hardened web-fetch (with SSRF-guard and prompt-injection defenses) that lets the entity look things up independently, and a read-only self-reflection tool giving it access to its own observability data so it can examine its past performance from within a conversation. General-purpose task skills are injected into the motor cortex's planning and execution prompts, providing domain-relevant know-how for the task at hand.

5. Implementation Status

The system is fully operational with all major clusters implemented. The codebase includes:

  • All brain-region clusters (frontal, temporal, parietal, occipital, hippocampus, hypothalamus, thalamus, brainstem, plus motor and auditory cortex), built on shared switch-neuron and integrator-cell base classes
  • The full predict-and-surprise gating system, including the frontal composite predictor
  • The plastic wiring graph with decay and history snapshots
  • The two-tier chemical bus (fast neuromodulators + slow hormones) with cross-channel interaction
  • The long-term store with vector-indexed episodic and human-readable schema layers
  • The identity and persona system, with sleep consolidation revising both the self-schema and per-speaker profiles
  • The default mode network, metacognition, and sleep consolidation (including the REM-style idle-thought pass)
  • Voice I/O (streaming speech-to-text and text-to-speech with deliberate emotional expression)
  • The auditory cortex with speaker enrollment, per-speaker prosody baselines, and song fingerprinting
  • The motor cortex with sandboxed tool execution, the self-directed task system, the hardened web-fetch and read-only self-reflection tools, and task-skill injection
  • Both skill libraries (self-reflection and general-purpose task) with tiered baseline injection, sticky-context reuse, and hybrid embedding/LLM selection
  • A full observability stack: event logging, browser UI, and a batch evaluation pipeline
  • A comprehensive automated test suite and CI with linting and security scanning

The system boots from a single shell script and runs in multiple feature configurations (minimal text, standard, full stack with voice).

6. Open Research Questions

This system was built to answer specific questions. The following are the central empirical bets the architecture makes, in order of priority.

Does affective gating produce measurably different behavior? The core hypothesis is that genuine affective architecture — neuromodulator state that persists across turns and modulates computation — produces behavior that differs from a single well-prompted LLM in ways that matter: not necessarily better on raw task quality, but different in character. The architectural prediction is behavioral continuity and emotional coherence over long windows. The test requires blinded, compute-matched comparison over enough turns to let the chemical state and plasticity history diverge meaningfully from a stateless baseline. This has not yet been run at scale.

Is structural affect distinguishable from a convincing description of it? This is the sharpest version of the central bet and the claim this report is most careful not to overstate. The system computes from genuine internal state rather than an injected description (§2.3); whether that mechanistic difference is perceptible to users, and whether it changes their behavior (openness, disclosure, sustained engagement), is unknown. The §2.4 literature establishes that perceived emotional responsiveness and continuity matter, but not that genuine state outperforms a sufficiently good description of one. A controlled comparison against a strong context-injection baseline is the test, and it is the experiment the architecture most exists to run.

Does character develop over time, and do separate instances diverge? The identity schema provides the infrastructure for character development: plastic weights that reinforce preferred paths, neuromodulator baselines that encode accumulated emotional context, and a self-narrative revised each session. The prediction is that an entity run over hundreds of sessions shows measurable trajectory in its emotional range, its reinforced paths, and its own self-description. The sharper, more falsifiable form is divergence: two instances started from identical code and run with two different people (or two different interaction regimes) over the same period should end measurably further apart than two instances given identical histories. This is a clean A/B test of the per-relationship thesis (§1) — if identical code plus different lives does not produce different entities, the claim fails. A cross-session identity evaluator provides the measurement.

Does predict-and-surprise gating reach its efficiency target, and can the savings be reallocated? The design target is a substantial rate of integrator suppression on a mature predictor with sufficient session history, constrained primarily by session length. Beyond the suppression rate, the stronger hypothesis is that saved budget can be explicitly redirected — turning a suppressed routine call into headroom for more drafters, richer recall, or a more capable model on the high-surprise cases in the same session — producing output-quality gains on the cases that most need them. Both are testable by comparing save rates across session lengths and output quality on high-surprise turns with and without active reallocation.

Does plasticity produce emergent preferences and experience-driven specialization? The theoretical prediction is that preferred drafters, recall paths, and switch orderings emerge from reinforcement over many sessions. The outcome signal is designed to produce both strengthening and weakening of paths; whether weakening actually materializes in benign sessions, and whether reinforcement produces a measurable behavioral preference, requires the dataset to grow. The thesis-level form of this question is specialization (§1): the prediction is not that the system beats a single well-prompted model on arbitrary tasks, but that an instance with concentrated history in one domain or with one person comes to outperform a fresh instance — and its own earlier self — on tasks of that kind. The test is a longitudinal, within-domain quality comparison against a stateless baseline held at equal compute, which separates genuine specialization from base-model capability.

Does emotional state actually govern what is learned? The coupling claim (§1, §4.3) is that affect sets the learning rate and the reward signal, so that emotionally salient turns reshape the system more than flat ones. This is directly testable from the decision log: the per-turn weight change should correlate with the affective intensity of the turn, and ablating the affect-derived modulator should flatten that correlation and slow the emergence of preferences. If weight change is independent of emotional state, the "what it feels shapes what it becomes" claim is false for this implementation.

Does the novelty signal decline with familiarity? The acetylcholine channel encodes novelty, so sessions that increasingly revisit familiar patterns should show an attenuating novelty signal over time — which would be early evidence of long-horizon environmental adaptation. The confound is session-content uniformity; controlled session diversity is the test.

Does the general-purpose skill library improve motor-cortex task quality? Injecting domain-relevant skills into planning and execution is hypothesized to improve task quality, particularly for tasks outside the base model's strongest domains. A blinded comparison with and without skill injection on matched tasks is the test.

Do the relationship mechanisms produce measurable bonding effects? The human-AI interaction literature (§2.4) establishes that persistent emotional state, memory continuity, and reciprocal self-disclosure produce measurable effects on user attachment, disclosure, and satisfaction in the systems studied. This architecture implements those mechanisms, but whether they produce the same effects here requires longitudinal measurement. Three predictions follow: users who interact across many sessions should show increasing self-disclosure correlated with oxytocin accumulation and affection growth; the empathy critic's contribution to draft selection should correlate with user satisfaction; and proactive surfacing of episodic memory should produce measurable rapport response. The feature directions identified in §2.4 are design targets whose value is predicted by the literature and testable once implemented.

7. Known Design Constraints

Consensus on garbage. The critic cell and multi-draft tournament provide some protection against low-quality output, but if all active drafters share a systematic hallucination, the architecture has no principled remedy. This is a known open problem in multi-agent systems generally.

Latency. The sequential LLM call chain on the critical path imposes a latency floor. Simple conversational turns are fast; complex multi-drafter and autonomous task turns are substantially longer. The system was designed with the expectation that latency is a primary UX limitation, and the real-time brain-activation visualization is meant to frame deliberation as evidence of live processing rather than a loading state.

Calibration brittleness. A large set of hand-tuned constants governs thresholds, decay rates, and activation boundaries, and the boundaries that convert continuous neuromodulator state to discrete labels are sensitive to small drift. Principled calibration — sensitivity analysis, ablation passes, a calibration regression harness — is a known backlog item. This brittleness is also part of why the system is hard to reconstruct from a description alone (§2.3).

Single-user architecture. The current implementation is single-tenant by design. Multi-user deployment would require per-session state scoping and an auth layer. This is not a current goal; the research questions concern longitudinal single-entity development, not throughput.

Replay determinism. Exact replay is not attempted — async and LLM nondeterminism make it impossible. The logging is designed for reconstruction and analysis, not deterministic replay.

The central open question. Whether the multi-agent architecture produces better responses than a single well-prompted LLM given equal compute is not yet answered. The honest prior from the literature is that multi-agent structure adds legibility and behavioral structure but does not reliably beat a single good model on raw response quality. The architecture's differentiated value — if it has one — is more likely to appear in behavioral continuity and character development over long windows than in per-turn response quality.

8. Relation to the Philosophy of Mind Literature

Because the philosophical commitments are treated as design specifications (§3.1), they can be evaluated against the running system rather than merely asserted.

Functionalism: The system's operation is genuinely substrate-independent. The same conversation has been run with multiple different model backends — a hosted frontier model, a hosted fast model, and a local open-weight model — as the integrators. The behavioral differences are stylistic, not structural. The functional organization — switch gating, convergence events, drafter tournaments — operates identically regardless of which models run the integrators.

Dual-Process: The switch/integrator distinction cleanly separates fast-and-automatic from slow-and-deliberate processing. Whether this produces the specific phenomenological properties Kahneman attributes to System 1 and System 2 in humans is a category error to ask — but the computational analogy is genuine. The system literally does not reason about routine inputs; it pattern-matches. It literally does reason about novel or high-surprise inputs.

Global Workspace: The message bus with its attention-focus channel implements a workspace in the Baars/Dehaene sense: local processing is invisible to other clusters, and promotion to the focus channel makes content available system-wide. This is architectural, not metaphorical.

Multiple Drafts: The drafter tournament is genuinely draft-parallel. Multiple integrators write candidate responses without knowledge of each other's drafts. The critic scores them. The articulation gate emits the winner. There is no "true response" that was waiting to be discovered — only the drafts that existed when the gate fired.

Extended Mind: The external store satisfies Clark and Chalmers' coupling and availability conditions: reliably available, automatically endorsed, directly accessible. The entity's responses are shaped by its content on a substantial fraction of turns. The notable extension: this store is non-degrading, making it a stronger version of the extended mind than the original Otto/Inga case, which assumed a compensatory external memory for biological forgetting.

Narrative Self: The identity schema provides the substrate for the Lockean claim that personal identity is memory continuity. The entity's self-model is explicitly maintained as a document it treats as its own history, revised by the entity itself rather than by external instruction. The self is not imposed — it is accumulated.

9. Conclusion

This system is an implementation of biologically-inspired cognitive architecture at a scale and fidelity not previously published in the LLM multi-agent literature. Its core design claims — sparse LLM activation at convergence zones only, neuromodulator dynamics as free persistent state, hippocampus-gated memory with a vector episodic store, plasticity in edge weights, predict-and-surprise gating from Active Inference, and a persistent character identity revised by sleep consolidation — are not merely described but implemented and operational.

The most distinctive architectural commitment is the bet that affect is not decoration. Every other multi-agent LLM system treats emotional register as a property of the prompt. This system treats affect as state: chemical channels that persist across turns, that modulate computation through genuine inhibitory and excitatory pathways, and that accumulate over a long enough window to produce something that might be recognized as temperament. The slow hormonal tier is designed for a relationship that develops over months, not minutes. The second distinctive commitment is methodological: philosophy of mind is used as an engineering specification rather than as decoration, with each component derived from a position the running system can be held accountable to (§8).

From the bet that affect is state, three predictions follow, and together they are the project's thesis. First, because the system relates to each person out of their shared history rather than from a global default, it should behave differently toward different people — and two copies of identical code, given different lives, should diverge into measurably different entities rather than remaining one system with different notes attached. Second, because the same affective machinery that colors a response also sets how strongly that turn is learned, emotion and learning are not separable: what the system feels decides what it becomes. Third, because that learning accumulates as experience does in people, it should produce specialization — not a system that beats a single well-prompted model on an arbitrary task, but one that grows better, over time, at the specific tasks and relationships it has lived in. None of the three is demonstrated yet; each is stated as a falsifiable claim with a corresponding test (§6).

The honest characterization of the current state: this is a working research instrument with the right architecture and the wrong amount of data. The most important claim — that genuine internal affect produces behavior a user can tell apart from a convincing performance of it — is a hypothesis the system is built to test, not a result it has demonstrated. The mechanisms are active. The experiment is ongoing.
