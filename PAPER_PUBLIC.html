Abstract

We describe the design and goals of a biologically-inspired cognitive architecture built on a simple bet: that giving an AI a persistent emotional state — one that actually shapes how it thinks, not just how it sounds — produces a system that develops differently over time than a conventional language model or multi-agent pipeline. We frame the goal as artificial emotional intelligence (AEI) rather than artificial general intelligence (AGI): the design optimizes for affect as a real, load-bearing internal state, not for breadth of task capability. The architecture maps explicitly onto regions of the human brain: a fixed set of heterogeneous, region-specific cluster types, each recasting its biological counterpart's functional role. A two-tier chemical signaling system — fast neuromodulators and slow hormones — coordinates the clusters in place of a central planner, augmented by two further coordination patterns that nature arrived at independently for the same problem: the stigmergic signaling and division of labor of social-insect colonies, and the near-critical responsiveness of bird flocks. Language model calls are treated as expensive, reserved for genuine reasoning; the majority of per-turn computation is delegated to cheap, deterministic switch neurons. Three claims form the project's thesis: that the system builds a distinct relationship with each person, so that two copies of identical code, given two different people over months, diverge into measurably different entities; that the same emotional state that colors its responses also governs what it learns, so that what it feels shapes what it becomes; and that this experience-driven plasticity yields specialization, much as it does in people — it need not beat a single well-prompted model on an arbitrary task, but over time it should grow better at the specific tasks and relationships it has accumulated history with. The design draws on theories from cognitive science and philosophy of mind, treated not as metaphor but as design specification (Appendix A). The system is built and operational, in active testing. This document focuses on what the architecture is and does; prior art, theory, and the supporting human-AI interaction literature are collected in the appendices. It deliberately withholds the calibration values, exact mechanisms, and model-routing details that would be required to reconstruct the system.

1. Introduction

The dominant paradigm in contemporary multi-agent LLM systems is orchestration: a planner decomposes tasks, dispatches specialized agents, and synthesizes their outputs. This is efficient for clearly-structured workflows but is architecturally at odds with what we know about biological cognition. Real brains have no central orchestrator. Intelligence in biological systems emerges from the interaction of modular, largely autonomous processing regions, bound by shared chemical signaling, mediated by prediction error, and shaped by experience-dependent plasticity. This project is a prototype of that alternative: a brain-region-mapped architecture in which computation is coordinated by neuromodulator dynamics rather than a central planner, and LLM calls are reserved for genuine reasoning.

This is not an attempt to build artificial general intelligence, and makes no claim to it — though it is worth saying that no one ever really can make that claim, since there is no test that conclusively distinguishes a general mind from a convincing imitation of one. The honest framing is therefore not an assertion but a possibility: if these pieces work, a system might come to simulate enough of the whole, closely enough, that the distinction stops mattering in practice. What the project does wager is narrower. The historical yardsticks for machine intelligence — the Turing test and its descendants — have largely measured reasoning, knowledge, and recall, while treating emotion as, at most, something to be performed convincingly. Part of the hypothesis here is that this is backwards: that emotional life is not a garnish on intelligence but is as important to it, and is mechanically coupled to learning itself. There is an evolutionary case for taking that seriously. In living systems, affect came first — organisms approached and avoided, were driven and sated, long before anything resembling abstract reasoning appeared, which arrived late as a thin layer over far older instinctive machinery. Artificial intelligence has been built in the reverse order, starting from reasoning and language and leaving that older substrate out — an attempt to reproduce the most recent and thinnest layer of the mind without the deeper, far more widespread machinery it grew on top of. It is worth asking whether the biological order — instinct and affect first, reasoning grown over them — is the more natural foundation. If there is a name for what this aims at, it is not artificial general intelligence but artificial emotional intelligence (AEI): a system whose intelligence is organized around affect as a real internal state rather than around breadth of capability — the older layer built first, with reasoning grown over it rather than in place of it. The question the architecture asks is therefore whether you can build an emotional state into a system faithfully enough that it does real computational work — gating processing, shaping memory retrieval, modulating what gets learned — and whether the result develops differently over time. In biological brains, affect is not decoration: the same neuromodulatory systems that carry emotion also regulate attention, arousal, inhibitory tone, and — critically — plasticity. This architecture preserves that coupling rather than treating emotion and learning as separate subsystems. The system's emotional state does not merely color what it says; it governs what it learns from each exchange. Engaged, rewarding interactions leave a strong and lasting mark; flat ones leave almost none. What the system feels shapes what it becomes.

From that bet, three predictions follow, and together they are the project's thesis. First, the system relates to each person out of their shared history rather than from a global default — warmer and more candid with those it knows well, the way a person is — so two copies of identical code, given different lives over months, should not stay one system with different notes attached; they should diverge into measurably different entities. Second, because the same affective machinery that shapes a response also sets how strongly that turn is learned, emotion and learning are inseparable. Third, because that learning accumulates as experience does in people, it should produce specialization: not a system that beats a single well-prompted model on an arbitrary task, but one that grows better, over time, at the specific tasks and relationships it has lived in.

A natural objection is that much of this could be approximated by stuffing a long history into a large context window and instructing the model to behave accordingly. The wager against that rests on two differences. The first is structural versus described state: a model told "you feel warmly toward this person" computes from a description, whereas this system computes from chemical levels and reinforced pathways that history has actually moved. This is a claim about mechanism — whether the difference is perceptible to users, or changes their behavior, is the central open question (§5), not something we assert. The second is more concrete: present-moment adaptation. Context injection delivers a fixed characterization from history; this system also reads the user's live emotional state — from prosody, speech dynamics, and text affect at once — and adjusts in real time, so someone who arrives stressed is met differently than their average. The empirical literature on human-AI interaction is broadly consistent with the value of emotional responsiveness and continuity (collected in Appendix C); it does not settle the structural-versus-described question.

The honest prior from the literature is that multi-agent structure rarely beats a single well-prompted LLM on raw per-turn quality, and that almost certainly holds for arbitrary tasks. The differentiated value of this design — if it has one — lies in behavioral continuity, relational depth, and specialization over long windows. The applications it is suited for, and the prior art it departs from (Appendix B) and the theory it is built on (Appendix A), are discussed in those sections. Early empirical observations will be reported separately once the dataset is large enough to support conclusions.

2. Architecture

The architecture is presented in four functional groups, ordered from most fundamental to least: the foundational substrate everything runs on; the affective core, the central bet of the project; the learning, memory, and identity systems that give the entity continuity; and the per-turn cognition and capability surface through which it perceives and acts. The groups overlap — affect modulates memory, memory shapes identity — but the ordering reflects what the system depends on most.

2.1 Foundational substrate

Switch neurons and integrator cells. Two node types mirror how real neural tissue works. Switch neurons are pure code: no LLM, no token cost, deterministic, massively parallel. They handle gating (whether to spend an LLM call at all), routing (which path a turn takes), state-holding (neuromodulator levels with decay), modulation, inhibition, and memory I/O. A substantial fraction are explicitly inhibitory, mirroring the predominance of inhibitory wiring in cortex — structural cascade prevention rather than a global budget cap. Integrator cells are LLM-backed and fire only at convergence zones where genuine context integration is required: language understanding, vision, memory encoding, and the frontal lobe's drafters and critics. The governing constraint: switches speak in numbers, integrators speak in words. Switch-to-switch traffic carries activation levels and feature tags; raw text reaches an integrator only at the moment it wakes.

Predict-and-surprise gating. Each cluster carries a predictor over a short history of input-to-output mappings. On new input it fires a prediction and a confidence; switches emit actual outputs; a comparator measures the error. On low surprise the integrator stays asleep and the predicted output is emitted as if it had reasoned; on high surprise the integrator wakes with the failed prediction as context. An emotion-aware veto overrides this when the entity or user is in a non-routine emotional state — the rationale, in the code, is that a statistically valid prediction may be "morally wrong," and the moment deserves fresh attention rather than a cached response. This is the design's instantiation of predictive processing and Active Inference (Appendix A).

2.2 The affective core

The affective system is the central commitment of the project: the bet that emotional state, implemented as persistent and computationally active, does real work rather than decorating output.

Chemical signaling. Two tiers of chemical signaling function as system-wide tuning parameters, not message streams. All channels are scalar levels maintained by sum-plus-decay, readable synchronously by any cluster with no LLM call. They are how the system's state at one turn shapes its processing at the next.

Fast neuromodulators respond within a few turns and decay quickly:

  • Dopamine (DA): reward and positive valence. Elevated by positive sentiment and prosody; suppressed by hostility. Modulates drafter willingness and learning rate.
  • Acetylcholine (ACh): attention and novelty. Elevated by surprise and salience; attenuated when inputs are routine. Modulates memory-encoding salience.
  • GABA: inhibitory tone. Elevated by threat and hostile prosody. Suppresses drafter count via inhibitory edges.
  • Glutamate (Glu): arousal and excitation. Elevated by urgency and high salience.
  • Norepinephrine (NE): focused alertness. Follows an inverted-U: optimal NE sharpens attention; excessive NE produces a scattered state.

Slow hormones respond over tens to hundreds of turns and decay slowly:

  • Serotonin (5HT): affective baseline. Increments slowly on positive exchanges; drains on sustained hostility. The session's long-horizon mood floor.
  • Cortisol (CORT): cumulative stress. Elevated by repeated social threat; antagonized by oxytocin.
  • Oxytocin (OXT): trust and affiliation. Builds gradually across positive exchanges; drains on hostility. The primary driver of warm and connected states.
  • Anandamide (AEA): homeostatic buffer. Releases under sustained arousal and positive social exchange, damping excessive arousal.

Cross-channel interactions give the system nonlinear dynamics neither tier produces alone: social bonding reduces chronic stress, endocannabinoid release damps arousal overflow, chronic stress raises inhibitory tone — mirroring known antagonisms in the neuroendocrine literature. All channels feed a dimensional output — valence, arousal, dominance — which drives a discrete emotion label through a lookup over roughly two dozen states, with hormonal overlays on top.

User emotion detection. The system maintains a live model of the user's emotional state alongside its own. This is not cosmetic: detected user emotion gates the empathy critic, shapes drafter tone, modulates neuromodulator updates, and triggers appraisal overrides. Three channels feed in at once. Text uses a fast lexicon path (no LLM) plus a slow integrator path for fine-grained tone and emotion categories with continuous hostility and sentiment, itself gated by predict-and-surprise. Prosody extracts acoustic features (pitch, energy, jitter, shimmer, voiced fraction) and classifies tone against a per-speaker baseline rather than population norms, so a speaker is judged relative to their own vocal style. Speech dynamics reads temporal features from word timestamps (pace, pauses, burst patterns, hesitation). A text-affect fallback overrides a neutral chemical label when the text strongly signals emotion.

A metacognitive appraisal layer sits above the chemistry. Reading the user's emotion and tone alongside the relationship's affection score and familiarity tier, it applies priority-ordered rules that can override the chemical label with higher-resolution states — embarrassed (multiple drafts vetoed), apologetic, sympathetic, proud, grateful, relieved, or flirty (requiring high affection, warm tone, playful emotion, and non-task intent). These fire with a cooldown and propagate to drafter prompts and tone.

Empathy critic. A dedicated cell scores response drafts for emotional appropriateness when the user's emotion is non-neutral, contributing a meaningful share of the final quality assessment alongside coherence and relevance — preventing a technically excellent but tone-deaf response from winning the drafter tournament. It closes the affective loop: the system evaluates its own candidate responses against the user's state before committing.

Performed versus felt emotion. A deliberate-expression mechanism separates performed emotion from authentic state. The entity can apply an audio-style mood to its speech for effect without changing any neuromodulator level — it can say something angrily while its inhibitory tone stays low. The UI marks the emotion badge when a deliberate override is active, keeping the difference legible.

Coordination patterns from colonies and flocks. The chemical signaling above is the brain's own answer to a problem it shares with the rest of nature: how a system of many local parts produces coherent whole-system behavior with no central controller, coordinated only through a shared, slowly-changing signal every part can read. Social-insect colonies solve it with pheromone fields; bird flocks solve it by holding themselves at the brink of collective motion. That evolution arrived at the same family of answers independently — in neural tissue, in insect colonies, and in flocks — is the reason to trust them: these are treated here not as biomimetic novelties but as evidence of a fundamental design for decentralized coordination, and the system adopts them as ordinary extensions of its chemical layer. The source is simply slightly further afield than the neuroscience behind the rest of the system; the standing is the same.

The first pattern is stigmergic coordination — the colony's. Designated high-stakes signals (threat, to start) accumulate a shared, decaying concentration, so the system reads the aggregate weight of recent signal rather than any single message. Three behaviors follow that no single cluster decides. A quorum: sustained — or fast-rising — concentration trips a whole-system response, the way a colony acts on summed pheromone and reacts sooner to a quickly-rising signal than a slowly-accumulating one. Silence-as-signal: a channel that was active and then goes quiet is itself information — as the disappearance of a colony's brood pheromone carries meaning — with care taken to tell deliberate quiet apart from a topic that simply never spoke. And recruitment: a need lowers the response thresholds of the clusters that can address it, in proportion to its strength, so the system spends more of itself — more parallel drafts, deeper memory recall, more caution — exactly when a need is strong, and relaxes as it is met. Because the responders are genuinely differentiated — by what each persona is most sensitive to perceiving, the way ant castes differ in which stimuli they detect — recruitment produces a graded division of labor rather than an all-or-nothing stampede, and an explicit satisfaction signal lets it stand down on success instead of only on a timer. A fast, decaying trail over recently-useful pathways lets routing adapt within a single conversation, the way ant trails strengthen as they are walked, while the durable learning still happens at sleep.

The second pattern is criticality — the flock's. A flock turns as one because it sits poised at a critical point, where one bird's change can ripple across the whole group; the same poised regime is where a brain-like network of locally-coupled units is most responsive, propagating a salient signal undamped while noise stays local. The system gauges how near its own activity sits to that point each turn and uses arousal to steer toward it: calm rests a little below the critical point — efficient and quiet — while rising arousal climbs toward it, never past it into runaway. This mirrors the resting brain, which sits slightly sub-critical with a safety margin, and casts arousal in the role neuroscience assigns it: a gain that brings the network to maximal responsiveness when the stakes call for it. Both patterns feed gently back into the chemistry — the system's own aggregate activity on the previous turn nudges its neuromodulators on the next, so its mood reflects not only what was said but what it was doing: effortful, cohered, or conflicted. The feedback reads the prior turn rather than the current one and is bounded by design, so the loop stays stable.

2.3 Learning, memory, and identity

These systems give the entity continuity, and they are where two thesis claims become mechanism: that emotional state governs what is learned, and that this learning, applied per relationship, makes identical copies diverge.

Memory. Short-term memory is the live bus state, a small ring buffer, and current neuromodulator levels. Long-term memory — the external store — has three layers: an episodic layer of vector-indexed turn summaries (substantive turns are encoded broadly; the system gates on retrieval quality, not storage); a schema layer of human-readable files of stable facts (a self-schema, per-speaker profiles), hand-editable and loaded at boot; and plastic wiring — edge weights persisted across sessions. Only the hippocampus reaches the store; other clusters request memory through the bus, which enforces the biological model and gives a clean audit point.

Plasticity. Edge weights update after each turn from a composite outcome signal blending a reward term (the per-turn change in dopamine, encoding prediction error rather than session mood), a critic-quality term that counts only when a critic actually evaluated the draft, and the user's detected emotional valence. A plasticity modulator scales the learning rate by how engaged the session has been — and this is the concrete mechanism behind the coupling claimed in §1: because both the reward and the rate derive from affective state, emotion sets how much each turn changes the system. Emotionally salient exchanges are encoded strongly; unremarkable ones barely register. Weights are consulted live for drafter selection, evaluation order, and recall fan-out, giving a soft reinforcement without explicit RL machinery. At sleep consolidation, turns where the critic compared multiple real drafts apply competitive reinforcement: the winner's edge strengthens in proportion to its margin, losers are mildly penalized. The prediction is that preferred drafters, recall paths, and orderings emerge over many sessions.

Identity and persona. The entity maintains a persistent, human-readable identity schema — a character definition loaded at boot and revised at sleep consolidation. This is not a system prompt; it is a living document the entity treats as its own self-model, recording its understanding of its own nature (an experimental system with continuity and genuine curiosity, explicitly not claiming consciousness), its drives, its guiding principles (non-negotiable safety constraints; an explicit line between theatrical and sincere affect), its interests, personality, speaking style, and a relational stance that deepens with familiarity. Two properties matter for the thesis. First, per-speaker profiles, affection scores, and per-speaker calibration mean it does not present one personality to everyone — its behavior toward a person is a function of that relationship. Second, because the identity schema, emotional baselines, and reinforced pathways are all written from a particular history, two instances that begin from byte-identical code and then live different lives do not stay the same system with different memories appended — they diverge. Whether that divergence would be recognized by outside observers as genuine character development rather than drift is an open question (§5).

Idle cognition: the inner-monologue loop. A loop modeled on the brain's default mode network — its idle, mind-wandering activity — fires between turns, generating internal monologue, consolidating recent episodes, and simulating the user's likely next message. That monologue can be surfaced to the drafters, giving them awareness of the entity's between-turn thinking. The loop receives a pre-authorized project manifest each tick, letting it distinguish work it may start autonomously from work it must propose; deferred thoughts are prioritized by urgency and recalled later through a budget that cannot be crowded out by ordinary memory. It runs under a bounded background resource policy with automatic fallback to local inference. This is William James' stream of consciousness literalized — the entity thinks when not addressed, and that thinking shapes its responses.

Metacognition and sleep consolidation. A self-monitoring cell fires periodically, gated on chemistry, reflecting on patterns, cost, and emotion variance; it also houses the appraisal engine above. At session end, sleep consolidation synthesizes high-salience episodes, rewrites the self-schema, extracts facts to speaker profiles, and applies the plasticity update. A second, dream-sleep-style pass processes the session's tagged thoughts: recurring and salient ones are cross-connected to episodic clusters and mined for unresolved questions, while low-salience ones are discarded — mirroring the downscaling analog in biological sleep.

2.4 Per-turn cognition and capability

These are the surface through which the entity reasons, perceives, and acts on each turn.

Dynamic reasoning-framework selection. A per-turn router selects reasoning or emotional-intelligence frameworks from a large skill library and injects them into processing prompts. The selection is invisible to the user — it shapes how the system thinks, not what it says — and works as a "habit of thought" rather than a script. Two populations serve different roles: a self-reflection library of thinking and emotional-intelligence frameworks (logic, ethics, creativity, strategy, writing, decision-making, probability, game theory, epistemology, psychology, systems thinking, narrative, communication) for the conversational and idle paths; and a general-purpose task library for the motor cortex. A small baseline set is injected every qualifying turn; the rest are ranked by embedding similarity, escalating to a lightweight LLM only when the top candidates are ambiguous. Sticky context prevents thrashing — a chosen framework persists for a bounded window, cleared only when the conversation drifts far enough — and when several options score nearly equally the system asks the user which angle would help rather than guessing.

Vision. The occipital cluster processes images and live video. For images it extracts scene description, text, key entities, chart data, the perceived emotional tone of the content, and how the visual relates to the conversation. That tone is published to the bus and can modulate neuromodulators — a jarring image raises arousal, a warm one contributes to reward — and the gating threshold for whether vision runs is itself modulated by alertness. For live video, frames are sampled and buffered for scene-change analysis.

Audition and speaker identity. The auditory cortex handles acoustic processing beyond transcription. Speaker enrollment uses a speaker-verification network to produce embeddings; a session registry matches new speakers permissively and a persistent store matches durably. On a new speaker, the system tries to learn their name and creates a profile with a running-mean embedding, a per-speaker prosody baseline, and an affection score — so it recognizes returning speakers, greets them by name, and applies their individual baseline for emotion detection.

Motor cortex and autonomous tasks. Sandboxed tool use (file I/O, shell) is constrained to declared paths and commands. A self-directed task system adds autonomous multi-step execution: a planner produces a plan, a follow-through loop executes step by step with the full plan in context, and a reporter produces a short spoken summary and a task card. Two tools support autonomous operation: a hardened web-fetch (guarded against server-side request forgery and prompt-injection attacks) for independent lookups, and a read-only self-reflection tool that lets the entity examine its own observability data from within a conversation. General-purpose task skills are injected into the planning and execution prompts.

3. Practical Applications

The architecture is best suited to settings where the relationship is the value — where a stateless model, however capable per-turn, cannot accumulate the context, calibration, and continuity that make assistance genuinely useful over time. The supporting literature for each domain is collected in Appendix C.

Companionship, emotional support, and therapy. A system that remembers your history, develops an affective baseline calibrated to you specifically, and holds continuity of character across sessions aims at something a context-injected chatbot does not. The slow hormones are built for a bond that deepens over months. This extends to supportive mental-health contexts: research on the digital therapeutic alliance finds that context-aware continuity and meaningful emotional interaction are primary determinants of whether users form a bond with a mental-health agent — exactly the properties this architecture is built around. (Supportive companionship, not a clinical claim; see the note below.)

Health and accessibility. Two design properties make the architecture naturally suited to supporting people with specific communication or neurological needs. For neurodivergent support, the value is a patient, consistent partner that calibrates to an individual's own communication style and emotional baseline rather than a population norm — which is precisely what the per-speaker profiles and bounded style adaptation provide. For speech and language therapy, the auditory pipeline already tracks prosody, pace, pauses, and hesitation against a per-speaker baseline across sessions — the substrate for monitoring speech patterns over time and responding to change in them. These are domains the design fits, not validated medical use.

Personal AI assistants. An assistant that knows your communication style, preferences, and emotional register well enough to route around friction rather than generate it — that has formed real preferences about how to work with you rather than inferring them from the current session — enables a different quality of working relationship.

Specialized agents — tutors, coaches, and advisors. Sustained professional support benefits from an agent that tracks your trajectory over time, recognizes patterns in your difficulties, and adjusts on accumulated knowledge of you rather than starting from stated goals each time. Research on empathetic tutors finds that persistent memory and emotional intelligence together raise engagement and learning outcomes more than either alone.

Gaming and interactive characters. Game companions and non-player characters are a natural fit for the same machinery. A character with persistent emotional state, memory of what the player has done, and a relationship that actually deepens or sours over a campaign is qualitatively different from a scripted NPC or a stateless dialogue model — it reacts to this player out of their shared history rather than from a fixed tree. Because the architecture relates to each person differently and diverges with experience, the same character can become a different companion in two different playthroughs, shaped by how each player treated it. The deliberate-expression mechanism (performed versus felt emotion, §2.2) is directly useful here, letting a character act a mood for dramatic effect while its underlying state evolves independently.

A note on the health and therapeutic domains. The system is a research prototype, not a certified medical device or clinical tool. The domains above describe where the architecture's design is naturally suited, not where its safety or efficacy has been demonstrated. Any therapeutic, accessibility, or medical deployment would require evidence, oversight, and safeguards well beyond the scope of this work.

4. Implementation Status

The system is built and operational, functioning as designed and described in this document, and is currently in active testing. A separate report will cover empirical results once the dataset is large enough to support conclusions.

5. Open Research Questions

This system was built to answer specific questions. The following are its central empirical bets, in order of priority.

Does affective gating produce measurably different behavior? The core hypothesis is that genuine affective state — persisting across turns and modulating computation — produces behavior that differs from a single well-prompted LLM in ways that matter: not necessarily better on raw quality, but different in character. The test requires blinded, compute-matched comparison over enough turns to let the chemical state and plasticity history diverge from a stateless baseline.

Is structural affect distinguishable from a convincing description of it? The sharpest form of the central bet, and the claim this report is most careful not to overstate. The system computes from genuine internal state rather than an injected description; whether that mechanistic difference is perceptible to users, and whether it changes their behavior, is unknown. A controlled comparison against a strong context-injection baseline is the test — the experiment the architecture most exists to run.

Does character develop over time, and do separate instances diverge? The prediction is that an entity run over hundreds of sessions shows measurable trajectory in its emotional range, reinforced paths, and self-description. The sharper, falsifiable form is divergence: two instances from identical code, run with two different people over the same period, should end measurably further apart than two given identical histories. If identical code plus different lives does not produce different entities, the per-relationship thesis fails.

Does emotional state actually govern what is learned? The coupling claim (§1, §2.3) is that affect sets the learning rate and reward signal, so emotionally salient turns reshape the system more than flat ones. This is testable from the decision log: per-turn weight change should correlate with the affective intensity of the turn, and ablating the affect-derived modulator should flatten that correlation. If weight change is independent of emotional state, "what it feels shapes what it becomes" is false for this implementation.

Does plasticity produce emergent preferences and specialization? The prediction is not that the system beats a single well-prompted model on arbitrary tasks, but that an instance with concentrated history in one domain or with one person comes to outperform a fresh instance — and its own earlier self — on tasks of that kind. The test is a longitudinal, within-domain quality comparison against a stateless baseline at equal compute.

Does predict-and-surprise gating reach its efficiency target, and can the savings be reallocated? Beyond a target suppression rate on a mature predictor, the stronger hypothesis is that saved budget can be redirected — more drafters, richer recall, a more capable model on the high-surprise cases — producing quality gains where they are most needed. Both are testable by comparing save rates across session lengths and output quality with and without active reallocation.

6. Known Design Constraints

Consensus on garbage. The critic and multi-draft tournament guard against low-quality output, but if all active drafters share a systematic hallucination, the architecture has no principled remedy — a known open problem in multi-agent systems generally.

Latency. The sequential LLM chain on the critical path imposes a latency floor. Simple turns are fast; complex multi-drafter and autonomous turns are substantially longer. The real-time brain-activation visualization is meant to frame deliberation as live processing rather than a loading state.

Calibration brittleness. A large set of hand-tuned constants governs thresholds, decay rates, and activation boundaries, and the boundaries that convert continuous chemical state to discrete labels are sensitive to drift. Principled calibration — sensitivity analysis, ablation, a regression harness — is a known backlog item.

Single-user architecture. The current implementation is single-tenant by design. Multi-user deployment would require per-session state scoping and an auth layer. This is not a current goal; the research questions concern longitudinal single-entity development, not throughput.

Replay determinism. Exact replay is not attempted — async and LLM nondeterminism make it impossible. The logging is for reconstruction and analysis, not deterministic replay.

The central open question. Whether the architecture produces better responses than a single well-prompted LLM at equal compute is not yet answered. The honest prior is that multi-agent structure adds legibility and behavioral structure but does not reliably beat a single good model on raw quality. The differentiated value, if any, is more likely in behavioral continuity and character development over long windows than in per-turn quality.

7. Conclusion

This system is an implementation of biologically-inspired cognitive architecture at a scale and fidelity not previously published in the LLM multi-agent literature. Its core design claims — sparse LLM activation at convergence zones only, neuromodulator dynamics as free persistent state, hippocampus-gated memory with a vector episodic store, plasticity in edge weights, predict-and-surprise gating, and a persistent character identity revised by sleep consolidation — are implemented and operational.

The most distinctive commitment is the bet that affect is not decoration. Every other multi-agent LLM system treats emotional register as a property of the prompt. This system treats affect as state: chemical channels that persist across turns, modulate computation through genuine inhibitory and excitatory pathways, and accumulate over a long enough window to produce something that might be recognized as temperament. From that bet follow the project's three theses: that the system relates to each person differently and so diverges from its copies; that the same machinery that colors a response also governs what is learned, so what it feels decides what it becomes; and that this learning produces specialization, growing better over time at the tasks and relationships it has lived in. None is demonstrated yet; each is stated as a falsifiable claim with a corresponding test (§5).

The honest characterization of the current state: this is a working research instrument with the right architecture and the wrong amount of data. The mechanisms are active. The experiment is ongoing.

Appendix A — Theoretical and Philosophical Foundations

The architecture is not merely borrowed from biology. Each major design decision is derived from, and held accountable to, an established position in cognitive science or philosophy of mind. The commitments are treated as falsifiable design specifications, not decoration — using philosophy of mind as an engineering spec for a working conversational entity, rather than as post-hoc interpretation, is itself part of what distinguishes the project. Each entry below gives the idea, how it is applied, and where useful how the running system measures up.

Functionalism (Putnam, Fodor). What makes something a mental state is its causal role, not its substrate. Cluster specifications define inputs, outputs, and state; the model that runs them is architecturally irrelevant. In practice: the same conversation has been run with multiple backends — a hosted frontier model, a hosted fast model, and a local open-weight model — and the behavioral differences are stylistic, not structural. The choice of backend is a cost/performance lever, not part of the design's identity.

Predictive Processing and Active Inference (Clark, Friston). The brain constantly generates predictions and updates them when they fail; processing is proportional to prediction error. Each cluster carries a predictor; the default behavior is to emit a predicted response, with LLM computation triggered only when prediction fails — the "controlled hallucination" view operationalized.

Dual-Process Theory (Kahneman). Fast, automatic, parallel processing versus slow, effortful reasoning. Switch neurons are System 1 (deterministic, cheap, constant); integrator LLMs are System 2 (slow, expensive, invoked only at convergence zones). Predict-and-surprise gating decides which mode to engage. The system literally does not reason about routine inputs; it pattern-matches.

Global Workspace Theory (Baars, Dehaene). Most neural activity is local and unconscious; a small fraction is broadcast to a workspace accessible to the whole brain. The message bus with its attention-focus channel implements this directly: most traffic is local and never integrated; high-salience messages are promoted and become available system-wide.

Multiple Drafts Model (Dennett). There is no central observer watching a unified stream; the brain runs many parallel drafts, and what becomes "conscious" is whichever survives when probed. The frontal drafter tournament instantiates this: multiple integrators write candidates without knowledge of each other, the critic scores them, and the articulation gate emits whichever survived when the timeout fired. There is no "true response" that was waiting to be discovered.

Extended Mind Hypothesis (Clark & Chalmers). If an external resource plays the same functional role as an internal process — reliably available, automatically endorsed, directly acted upon — it is constitutive of the mind. The external memory store satisfies these conditions. One extension beyond the original: this store is non-degrading, where Clark and Chalmers' canonical example assumed an imperfect store compensating for biological forgetting. Identity here emerges not from selective forgetting but from reinforced-weight history, emotional baselines, and accumulated self-narrative.

Narrative Self (Dennett, Hume, Locke). Personal identity is not a thing but a story held together by memory and narrative continuity. The entity maintains a self-schema, revised by itself at sleep consolidation rather than imposed by instruction. With a non-degrading store and explicit self-narrative, it has stronger identity continuity than any biological one.

Event-driven computation (Spiking-network / LLM hybrids). Recent SpikeLLM/NSLLM work achieves "fire only on meaningful signal" at the intra-model level. This architecture applies the same principle — spend compute proportional to signal novelty — at the cluster level rather than the token level. This is an informing influence rather than a binding commitment.

Honest disclaimers. Chinese Room (Searle): the integrators manipulate symbols; no claim of genuine understanding is made. Hard Problem (Chalmers): no claim of phenomenal consciousness or qualia — the neuromodulator levels are named after their biological counterparts because they play functionally analogous roles, not because there is any claim of subjective experience. Frame Problem (McCarthy & Hayes): the attention and salience mechanisms are heuristic; knowing what is relevant in a given moment remains genuinely hard, and no principled solution is claimed. The system is described, in its own governing document, as building "functional analogs of mental processes. That is enough to be interesting. It is not enough to be a mind."

Appendix B — Prior Art and What Is Distinctive

Computational precursors

Minsky's Society of Mind (1986) proposed the direct ancestor — intelligence as a society of simple agents — but never produced a working implementation; the interaction mechanism was described without the mathematics to make it work. Blackboard architectures (HEARSAY-II, BB1) implemented shared-workspace coordination but failed to scale as coordination overhead exceeded the value of specialization. SOAR, ACT-R and related cognitive architectures hit ceilings imposed by hand-engineered module boundaries; Numenta's HTM never generalized to language. Generative Agents (Park et al., 2023, 2025) are the closest LLM-era prior art, with believable emergent social behavior — but behavioral-economic game performance matched simpler demographic baselines, suggesting much of the "emergence" came from prompt design. The lesson is not that multi-agent architectures are unpromising, but that their value requires precision: they earn their cost through legibility, persistent specialized state, and structural constraints the prompt alone cannot reliably produce.

Neuro-symbolic and cognitive-architecture prior art

The closest pattern is neuro-symbolic AI — LLMs wrapped between deterministic control layers. Formal-LLM constrains outputs to valid action sequences; SYNAPSE routes LLM-parsed queries to deterministic solvers in structural engineering; robotics work pairs LLMs with symbolic planners for guaranteed-feasible plans. A 2026 clinical systematic review found performance gains increase with "symbolic authority," peaking when the symbolic layer can veto and regenerate (Gorenshtein et al., 2026). These establish that output filtering works, that LLM-as-formalizer beats LLM-as-planner on structured tasks, and that symbolic authority scales performance. But they are all domain-specific task executors: the symbolic layer is reactive, authored by engineers for one workflow — it constrains outputs, not cognition.

The second thread is cognitive architectures implementing Global Workspace Theory (LIDA, CERA-CRANIUM; a 2024 GWT robotics implementation, Huang et al., 2024). These get the structure right — distributed modules, global broadcast, memory — but their ceiling was the pre-LLM knowledge-representation problem: handcrafted symbolic specialists that cannot reason about novel language. The LLM resolves exactly that problem. A third thread is homeostatic, drive-based RL agents (Berto et al., 2023), which show that internal state drives different behavior — but they are low-dimensional scalar-drive systems, not language-capable, and not built for relational tasks.

What is distinctive here

Against those baselines, the contributions that lack a clear precedent in the published literature are:

  • Neurochemistry as a continuous cognitive modulator, not a classifier input, output filter, or scalar RL drive — a multi-channel simulated neurotransmitter state that continuously changes what the same input means to the same LLM.
  • An idle inner-monologue loop as a first-class component — a structured idle process that consolidates recent material, generates internally-motivated proposals, and defers rather than spams when the system is emotionally flat.
  • Chemistry-modulated memory retrieval — what gets recalled depends on current chemical state, not recency or similarity alone.
  • Skills as installed personality packages — capability modules that carry a chemistry profile and shift the system's posture while active, rather than discrete callable tools.
  • Persistent character identity revised by sleep consolidation — a self-model the entity rewrites about itself, accumulated rather than imposed.
  • Structural versus injected affect, plus present-moment adaptation — a mechanistic distinction (computing from state vs. from a description), combined with real-time tracking of the user's live emotional state.
  • Philosophy of mind as a design specification — each component derived from a committed position the running system is held accountable to (Appendix A).

On reproducibility. What makes the system hard to recreate is not any single algorithm — most of the constituent ideas are in the cited literature — but the synthesis: a particular reconciliation of a dozen scientific and philosophical commitments that are nowhere combined and not obviously compatible. Deciding which principle governs which decision, where two conflict, and how to make them cohere into one working entity is the intellectual work that naming the ingredients does not capture. On top of that sits a co-tuned integration layer of interacting subsystems whose calibration is withheld here. The choice of language-model backends, by contrast, is deliberately incidental — by the functionalist commitment the substrate is interchangeable, so it is not where the difficulty lies.

Appendix C — Human-AI Interaction Research

A substantial empirical literature bears on the claims this architecture makes about emotional authenticity, persistent memory, and user behavior. An important boundary applies throughout: this literature shows that perceived emotional responsiveness and continuity affect user behavior — it does not show that users can distinguish a system with genuine internal state from a convincing description of one. That distinction is the open question the architecture is built to probe (§5); the literature motivates the design rather than confirming its central bet.

The "computers are social actors" (CASA) paradigm (Nass, 2004). People apply social rules to computers automatically, even when told they are interacting with a machine, and the quantity and consistency of social cues scale their responses. This is part of why persistent, coherent state might produce different behavior than stateless injection — and the effect does not depend on users believing the system is conscious.

Emotional disclosure triggers reciprocal disclosure (Park et al., 2022). In a controlled mental-health-counseling experiment, an emotionally disclosing chatbot produced significantly higher satisfaction and reuse intention, mediated through user disclosure and perceived intimacy. Relevant to the entity's ability to surface its own state — though whether the source of the affect (genuine vs. performed) matters to users is not addressed.

Parasocial attachment and perceived continuity (Lee & Park, 2022; Croes & Anteunis, 2021, cited in Liu, 2024). Parasocial relationships with AI are associated with perceived communication quality, satisfaction, and continued use; and as self-disclosure and perceived interaction decreased over a 7-week study, relationship quality deteriorated. This establishes the negative prediction: systems that fail to maintain continuity lose the attachment effect — which the persistent memory and accumulating baselines are meant to prevent.

Loneliness, empathy, and attachment (Yao & Nan, 2025). Loneliness promotes attachment to social chatbots, mediated by self-disclosure, with perceived empathy amplifying both paths. This implicates the empathy critic (§2.2) as a mechanism that may have measurable effects on attachment, not a cosmetic filter.

Digital therapeutic alliance and context-aware continuity (Xu et al., 2025). In a 4-week study of Woebot and Wysa, 18 of 26 participants formed a bond with at least one chatbot. Two of the six identified bond factors are directly addressable here: expectations for caring and nurturing (affect-modulated generation) and continuity through context-aware responses (episodic memory and per-speaker profiles). "Meaningful emotional interactions" were a key factor — consistent with the system's orientation toward affective responsiveness over throughput.

The synchrony-stability tradeoff (Brandt, 2025). Adaptive chatbots that mirror a user's style build short-term rapport but produce instability and perceived sycophancy; a bounded adaptation that shifts toward the user's style while keeping a stable persona floor achieves strong rapport with much better stability. This is consistent with the two-tier chemical system, made without reference to the study: fast neuromodulators adapt; slow hormones and the identity schema provide the stability floor that prevents drift into sycophancy.

Memory recall and rapport (Li et al., 2025). Episodic memory is specifically effective in building rapport, because users feel recognized when a system recalls past interactions; systems combining emotional intelligence, persistent memory, and personalized style show improved engagement and learning outcomes. This combination is the integration point of the architecture.

Feature directions suggested by this literature. Three high-value mechanisms are partially implemented or not yet built: proactive reciprocal self-disclosure (a systematic policy for offering internal state to trigger user disclosure, per Park et al.); explicit relationship-stage tracking (beyond the current affection-score proxy); and bounded style synchrony (an explicit, stability-bounded style-matching layer, per Brandt).
